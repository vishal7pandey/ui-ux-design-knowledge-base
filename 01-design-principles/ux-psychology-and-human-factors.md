# UX Psychology and Human Factors

Understanding how people think, perceive, remember, and act makes for better interfaces. Human factors engineering considers the limits of attention, memory, perception, and motor control.

---

## 1. Attention

Users have limited attention. Interfaces compete with distractions, goals, and habits.

### Key ideas

- **Selective attention**: users focus on one thing at a time.
- **Sustained attention**: attention drops over time.
- **Inattentional blindness**: users miss things outside their focus.

### Design implications

- Make the most important thing the most visible.
- Reduce distractions on task-critical screens.
- Use progressive disclosure to focus attention.
- Do not rely on users noticing subtle cues.

---

## 2. Memory

Working memory is limited to about 4–7 chunks. Long-term memory is large but fallible.

### Key ideas

- **Working memory**: temporary, limited storage.
- **Recognition over recall**: recognizing is easier than remembering.
- **Chunking**: grouping information into meaningful units.
- **Spacing effect**: learning improves with spaced repetition.

### Design implications

- Use labels, not placeholders.
- Show recently used options.
- Group related information.
- Provide reminders and defaults.
- Avoid making users remember things between screens.

---

## 3. Perception

Users perceive patterns, colors, motion, and contrast automatically.

### Key ideas

- **Gestalt principles**: proximity, similarity, closure, continuity, common fate.
- **Color contrast**: low contrast is harder to see.
- **Motion**: draws attention but can be distracting.
- **Visual hierarchy**: size, color, position, and spacing guide attention.

### Design implications

- Group related items visually.
- Use contrast to show hierarchy.
- Use motion purposefully.
- Make interactive elements obvious.

---

## 4. Decision-Making

Users make quick, often irrational decisions.

### Key ideas

- **Cognitive biases**: mental shortcuts that can mislead.
- **Choice overload**: too many options reduce decision quality.
- **Defaults**: users often accept pre-selected options.
- **Loss aversion**: losses feel worse than equivalent gains.

### Design implications

- Simplify choices.
- Use smart defaults.
- Frame options clearly.
- Reduce unnecessary decisions.

---

## 5. Motor Control

Users have physical limits for speed, precision, and fatigue.

### Key ideas

- **Fitts’s Law**: larger, closer targets are faster to hit.
- **Thumb zones**: some screen areas are easier to reach on mobile.
- **Fatigue**: long or repetitive tasks tire users.

### Design implications

- Make touch targets large.
- Place common actions in easy-to-reach areas.
- Reduce clicks and scrolling.
- Support keyboard and voice.

---

## 6. Human Factors Best Practices

- Design for the limits of attention and memory.
- Reduce cognitive load.
- Make information findable and scannable.
- Use consistent, familiar patterns.
- Provide clear feedback.
- Support recovery from error.
- Respect fatigue and physical limits.

---

## 7. Common Mistakes

- Overloading users with information.
- Hiding key information in dense text.
- Relying on users to remember.
- Too many choices or options.
- Small, hard-to-hit targets.
- Ignoring cognitive biases.
- Not considering fatigue or repetition.

---

## 8. Checklist

- [ ] The most important content is the most visible.
- [ ] Distractions are minimized on critical tasks.
- [ ] Information is chunked and grouped.
- [ ] Users are not asked to remember across screens.
- [ ] Labels and defaults support recognition.
- [ ] Contrast and hierarchy guide attention.
- [ ] Choices are limited and clearly framed.
- [ ] Touch targets are large and well-placed.
- [ ] Key actions are easy to reach on mobile.
- [ ] Feedback and recovery are clear.
