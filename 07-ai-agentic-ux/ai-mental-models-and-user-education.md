# AI Mental Models and User Education

Users form mental models of how AI works. If those models are wrong, users may over-trust, under-trust, or misuse the product. User education helps set accurate expectations.

---

## 1. Common AI Mental Models

### The oracle

Users think the AI knows everything and is always right.

**Risk**: over-trust, no verification.

**Design response**: show uncertainty, provide sources, encourage verification.

### The assistant

Users see the AI as a helpful but limited assistant.

**Risk**: may expect too much or too little.

**Design response**: set capability boundaries, demonstrate strengths and weaknesses.

### The human

Users think the AI has feelings, intentions, or personality.

**Risk**: emotional attachment, unrealistic expectations.

**Design response**: avoid anthropomorphizing, be clear the AI is a tool.

### The magic box

Users think the AI is incomprehensible and cannot be questioned.

**Risk**: blind trust or fear.

**Design response**: explain in plain language, show reasoning, make outputs verifiable.

---

## 2. Shaping the Right Mental Model

- Be honest about what the AI can and cannot do.
- Show the AI as a tool, not a person.
- Make AI behavior understandable and predictable.
- Provide feedback on how the AI works.
- Show the AI’s reasoning and sources.
- Allow users to correct the AI.

---

## 3. User Education Patterns

### Onboarding

- Explain the AI’s purpose, limits, and how to interact.
- Provide a quick, safe first task.
- Show examples.

### Tooltips and coach marks

- Highlight features and explain how they work.
- Use short, contextual messages.
- Let users dismiss and revisit.

### Capability statements

- “I can summarize documents up to 100 pages.”
- “I may make mistakes. Always verify important facts.”

### Limitation notices

- “I cannot access the internet.”
- “My knowledge is current as of March 2025.”

### Example prompts

- Show what users can ask.
- Help them learn to phrase requests.
- Update based on usage.

### Interactive demos

- Let users try the AI in a safe environment.
- Provide sample inputs and outputs.

### Explainable output

- Show how the AI reached a conclusion.
- Link to sources and reasoning.
- Highlight uncertainty.

---

## 4. Progressive Disclosure

- Start with simple, confident language.
- Offer more detail for users who want it.
- Avoid overwhelming users with disclaimers.
- Put advanced settings in a secondary location.

---

## 5. Best Practices

- Use plain language.
- Avoid AI jargon (training data, embeddings, parameters).
- Be specific about capabilities and limits.
- Reinforce the right model through the whole experience.
- Make uncertainty and sources visible.
- Encourage verification, especially for high-stakes tasks.
- Update users when the AI changes.

---

## 6. Common Mistakes

- Marketing the AI as all-knowing.
- Anthropomorphizing the AI.
- Hiding limitations in fine print.
- No examples or guidance.
- Overwhelming users with technical disclaimers.
- Not explaining why the AI did something.
- Failing to update users when capabilities change.

---

## 7. Checklist

- [ ] Capabilities and limits are clearly stated.
- [ ] The AI is described as a tool, not a person.
- [ ] Onboarding explains how to interact with the AI.
- [ ] Example prompts guide users.
- [ ] Uncertainty is communicated honestly.
- [ ] Sources and reasoning are shown.
- [ ] Limitation notices are in context, not hidden.
- [ ] Users are encouraged to verify important information.
- [ ] Language is plain and avoids jargon.
- [ ] Education is reinforced throughout the experience.
